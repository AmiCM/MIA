# The Language of Scent, Part 5

Category: Columns. Author: Miguel Matos.

Becoming a fragrance fan essentially requires one to learn a new language, and there is quite a bit of specialized terminology that we use when we talk about perfumes: from the process of creation all the way to how we wear them. Many of these terms can be easily mixed up and so Fragrantica's international writers present a 5 part series to help us all build our vocabulary and clarify misconceptions. Today, Fragrantica writer Miguel Matos who is also editor of the Fragrantica website in Portuguese , discusses 3 sets of often misunderstood terms. He is a vintage specialist, so retro, vintage and classic concepts are explored in this article as well as the natural elements of a perfume, be it factual or mere sensations. In the past weeks, we've presented 4 other sets of terms to help us better speak the language of scent. VINTAGE vs RETRO Vintage clock bottle of Guerlain Shalimar (1970's) vs modern Eris Parfum's Ma Bête (2016) Vintage is a fragrance that was produced a long time ago and is discontinued in its original version. It can be a fragrance that is still in production, but in a version that is no longer available. For example, Robert Piguet Bandit is still manufactured, but if you can get your hands on, say, a 1970's bottle, it's a much more glorious juice, if it was well preserved. The reason why vintage perfumes are more interesting is because they contain a lot of high-quality ingredients that are no longer allowed by the IFRA impositions. But vintage can also be much more recent. For example, Le Feu d'Issey , from 1998, is an extremely rare fragrance since it was discontinued shortly after its launch. It's also very expensive if you can find one specimen. Chanel nº5 , for example, is still in production but if you try a bottle that was made before the 1980's you can smell musks and other animalics that are no longer included in the formula. In short: vintage means a composition that is no longer in production, even if the perfume is still available in a newer reformulation. Retro is a perfume that tries to capture the feeling of the past. This is a fragrance that can even be new but it uses the codes of classic or old perfumery to convey a mood that is not contemporary. We can say that Dusita's masterpiece Le Sillage Blanc is a retro perfume because it goes back to the classic family of green leather chypres, inspired by Bandit and Cabochard, but is presented now. It looks at the past but it does that today, within today's regulations. Some new trends of iris fragrances can also be seen as retro since they look at an ingredient mostly associated with older perfumes and they convey an old-fashioned sensation, adapted to modern times. Eris Parfums , for example is a new brand that based its first line on the creation of vintage-inspired floral animalics. CLASSIC vs OLDFASHIONED A classic is a composition that stands the test of time. Perfumes that were an innovation like Chanel nº5, Guerlain Mitsouko or Mugler Angel they were all the rage when they were launched (well, Angel took a year to kick in), and they opened the doors to a new olfactive family. These perfumes tend to be considered icons and they live on, even when the fad is over. This is the proof that some fragrances are great in the past, present and future tense. They become classics, examples of that type of perfume and products that keep their allure for a very long time. Old-fashioned : Some perfumes are extremely successful at the time of launch only to fall into oblivion a few years after the trend has passed. They become obsolete and dated. These are that ones that the public loses interest in, fading slowly in the market and eventually getting discontinued. Some of them are still kept in production because there are still some buyers for it; generally people who wore it when it was trendy, because it can't get new consumers to like it. Unfortunately it seems that the green leather chypres tend to fall in this category nowadays, but I hope the floral fruitchoulis eventually get considered oldfashioned too. NATURAL CONTENT vs NATURAL SMELLING Ambrox molecule vs natural ambergris Natural content: Some perfumers insist on trying to create 100% natural perfumes. In my opinion, most of the time they fail to deliver a consistent formula that holds on to the skin with projection and stability. But there are exceptions, and very good ones, like Sven Pritzkoleit's Nardus and DoubleYou's Zanzibar for example. The perfect combination is a balance between synthetic and natural ingredients in perfumery. If synthetics are the backbones or the structure of a good perfume, natural ingredients are the flesh, the skin and hair of it. A mainly synthetic perfume is anorectic, it lacks richness and life that are the properties of natural notes. An example of natural vs synthetic is natural ambergris vs ambrox. If the first one is expensive and complex, the synthetic is cheaper and not as complex. Natural smelling: Some perfumes are not 100% natural and they can even be mainly synthetic. However, they can give a natural impression. This is because for almost every natural ingredient there is a molecule that is responsible for the major part of its smell. By isolating this molecule, it's possible to synthesize that scent, hopefully without losing its main character. A master perfumer is able to combine several synthetics in a complementary way in order to create an illusion of the natural material, thus creating a natural smelling perfume that can have just one or two drops of the real thing.
